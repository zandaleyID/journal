CTML and CATLM mostly focus on managing cognitive load. UDL starts with a different question, which is who can engage with content, and how (CAST, 2024). Putting the three side by side showed me that they do not always agree. Three tensions stood out: redundancy versus multiple representation, coherence versus engagement, and efficiency versus choice.

description: Each of three tensions sits between a CTML position on the left and a UDL position on the right, with a marker that starts closer to CTML. Redundancy versus multiple representation: with learner control off, captions and transcripts are cut as redundant, which lowers load but some learners lose access; with it on, learners switch captions and transcripts on or off for themselves. Coherence versus engagement: off, extras are cut to stay focused and learners get less connection to their own contexts; on, learners opt into richer, meaningful content when they want it. Efficiency versus choice: off, there is one fixed path that is efficient but inflexible; on, learners adjust the presentation or opt into pre-training, so choice does not mean everything is on for everyone. A closing box, the bridge, says to let learners decide how they access content.
[/embed]

One possible bridge, drawing on Fadel (2008), is to give learners control over how they access content, for instance, toggling captions, adjusting the presentation, or opting into pre-training. That meets UDL’s access goal without bringing back unnecessary load for everyone. I think of CTML as the lens for cognitive architecture, with CATLM extending it to cognitive-affective interaction, and UDL as the broader, inclusive design framework. It draws on CATLM’s affective ideas without collapsing into them (Moreno and Mayer, 2007; Ralabate, 2011).

## Meeting the tension in my own design
I also ran into this tension in PP2. I had drafted five learning outcomes for the orientation module, and each one held real value. However, looking at the whole wireframe layout, something felt off, and once I put myself in the facilitators’ position, it became clear that the content was too much.

These facilitators would already be navigating a new platform and a significant identity shift (Vendrell and Johnston, 2026). A task-heavy orientation on top of this risked the cognitive overload CTML warns about, and the disengagement CATLM predicts (Moreno and Mayer, 2007). The value of each activity risked going unrealised, the same wasted affordance I noted in my Coursera critique (Day and Lloyd, 2007). Merging two assessments and making the journal ungraded cut that extraneous load while keeping each outcome’s intent (Mayer, 2024).

## Final thoughts
Putting these frameworks side by side showed me that each theory can potentially fix a problem that the other can create. Giving learners some control over captions, presentation, and pre-training seemed to me a way to respect both, and my own overloaded draft showed me that instructional design is not only about how much valuable content you can include, but about how much a real learner, under real constraints, can actually carry. Even so, I am not sure how much choice is too much, and I would want to test it with real learners before relying on it entirely.
