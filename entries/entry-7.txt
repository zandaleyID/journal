Sessions 7 and 8 kept returning to one question: do the modes, activities and assessments actually work together, or is something in there just because it was available? I tested two of my PP2 decisions against it: the video comparison and the pedagogical agent.

## The video comparison
One learning outcome asks facilitators to tell apart a behaviourist, answer-checking response from a Socratic, guiding one. I used two videos for that, because the difference sits in tone, pacing and body language, which text cannot carry. This is what social semiotics describes: meaning is made across modes and changes when it moves between them (Kress et al., 2014). It also met the Session 7 criteria for choosing a mode. It serves the outcome, gives people new to the role something to model on, and shows what their real setting should look like. Facilitators then annotate the transcripts, which turns watching into analysing and gives them more than one way in.

## The gap I found
What I missed was accessibility. I chose video for the tone, pacing and body language, and a facilitator who cannot access the audio or visuals loses exactly those. The transcript gives the wording but none of the cues, and UDL’s principle of multiple means of representation (Ralabate, 2011) shows the gap. Adding stage directions such as “says slowly” would narrow it, but that describes the cue rather than carrying it, so it narrows the gap without closing it.

## The agent
In Unit 1 the agent is prominent, because that is where facilitators learn the platform through a guided walkthrough, and it is there to fill gaps the module cannot fill alone (Moreno and Mayer, 2007). In Units 2 to 4 it shrinks to a small icon, so facilitators know it is there but it is not front and centre. My main worry was behavioural: an always-prominent agent might become the answer to everything instead of an assistive tool.

The theory supports caution. Extraneous features can use up limited working memory (Moreno and Flowerday, 2006, cited in Moreno and Mayer, 2007), interactivity can pull attention to seductive details (Renkl and Atkinson, 2007), and an engaging affordance can become a distraction when the learning context lacks structure (Day and Lloyd, 2007). None of these sources addresses an agent’s size, so applying them here is my own design judgement.

## What this changed
I started with the agent throughout the whole module. Working through the theory showed me what that would cost, and the design is more considered for it. The theories pulled in different directions, but noticing the other direction is what showed me what was missing, whether that was the agent’s presence or the accessibility of my videos.

[question]
text: Think of a tool or assistant in an online course, such as a chatbot or a help button. Did it help you more when it was always visible, or when you could call on it only when you needed it?
reply: Thank you for sharing. When help is there for the choosing, it can feel quite different from help that is always in the way.
[/question]
