Entries 4 and 5 kept returning to the question of whether the modes, activities and assessments actually work together, or if something has been added simply because it was available. I revisited this through two PP2 design decisions, the video comparison and the pedagogical agent.

## The video comparison
One learning outcome asks facilitators to distinguish between behaviourist, answer-checking responses and Socratic, guiding responses. I proposed two videos because, as social semiotics suggests, each mode carries meaning differently (Kress et al., 2014), and tone, pacing, and body language communicate this contrast more effectively than text alone. The videos also provide realistic examples for novices to model (Ertmer and Newby, 2013), while transcript annotation turns viewing into active analysis.

## The gap I found
What I missed was accessibility. Facilitators unable to access the audio or visuals would miss some of the meaning conveyed by the videos (Ralabate, 2011). A transcript captures the wording, while stage directions only describe the missing cues. Captions with non-speech information, audio description, and a descriptive transcript could offer closer alternatives. I would also want to test these with the facilitators who rely on them. Although the alternatives may not be fully equivalent to video, the aim of UDL’s multiple means of representation is to ensure that everyone can access the same meaning, even through different experiences.

## The agent
In Unit 1, the agent is prominent because that is where facilitators learn the platform through a guided walkthrough, and it is there to provide support where the module falls short (Moreno and Mayer, 2007). In Units 2 to 4, it shrinks to a small icon, remaining available without dominating the learning experience. I was concerned that keeping it prominent throughout might encourage facilitators to rely on it for every answer rather than use it as an assistive tool.

The theory supports this caution. Extraneous features can use up limited working memory (Moreno and Mayer, 2007), and interactivity can pull attention to "seductive details" instead of key learning content (Garner, 1989, as cited in Renkl and Atkinson, 2007, p. 236). Engaging affordances can also become distractions when the learning context lacks structure (Day and Lloyd, 2007). None of these sources specifically addresses an agent’s size, so applying these principles to its visual prominence is my own design judgement.

## Final thoughts
I started with the agent throughout the whole module. Working through the theory revealed what that would cost, and the design is more considered for it. The theories pulled in different directions, but noticing the other direction is what showed me what was missing, whether that was the agent’s presence or the accessibility of my videos.

[question]
text: Think of a tool or assistant in an online course, such as a chatbot or a help button. Did it help you more when it was always visible, or when you could call on it only when you needed it?
reply: Thank you for sharing. When help is there for the choosing, it can feel quite different from help that is always in the way.
