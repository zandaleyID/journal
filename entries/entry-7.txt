Entries 4 and 5 kept returning to the question of whether the modes, activities and assessments actually work together, or if something has been added simply because it was available. I tested two of my PP2 decisions against this - the video comparison and the pedagogical agent.

## The video comparison
One learning outcome asks facilitators to differentiate between a behaviourist, answer-checking response and a Socratic, guiding one. I proposed two videos to show this contrast because the difference sits in tone, pacing, and body language, which text alone cannot convey as effectively. Social semiotics describes how meaning is made across modes and changes when it moves between them (Kress et al., 2014). The mode/s chosen should serve the outcome, give those new to the role something to model on, and represent their real setting. The activity concluded with facilitators annotating the transcripts, which turns watching into analysing and gives them more than one way to engage.

## The gap I found
What I missed was accessibility. I chose video for the tone, pacing, and body language, and a facilitator who cannot access the audio or visuals loses exactly those. The transcript gives the wording, but none of the cues, and UDL’s principle of multiple means of representation (Ralabate, 2011) shows the gap. Adding stage directions, such as “says slowly”, could narrow it, but that describes the cue rather than conveying it, which narrows the gap, but doesn't fully close it.

## The agent
In Unit 1, the agent is prominent because that is where facilitators learn the platform through a guided walkthrough, and it is there to assist facilitators where the module falls short (Moreno and Mayer, 2007). In Units 2 to 4, it shrinks to a small icon, so that facilitators know it is there but it is not front and centre. My concern was behavioural, and that an always-prominent agent might become the answer to everything instead of being used as an assistive tool.

The theory supports this caution. Extraneous features can use up limited working memory (Moreno and Mayer, 2007), and interactivity can pull attention to "seductive details" instead of important details for learning (Garner, 1989, as cited in Renkl and Atkinson, 2007, p. 236). Additonally, engaging affordance can become a distraction when the learning context lacks structure (Day and Lloyd, 2007). None of these sources addresses an agent’s size, so applying that logic here is my own design judgment.

## Final thoughts
I started with the agent throughout the whole module. Working through the theory revealed what that would cost, and the design is more considered for it. The theories pulled in different directions, but noticing the other direction is what showed me what was missing, whether that was the agent’s presence or the accessibility of my videos.

[question]
text: Think of a tool or assistant in an online course, such as a chatbot or a help button. Did it help you more when it was always visible, or when you could call on it only when you needed it?
reply: Thank you for sharing. When help is there for the choosing, it can feel quite different from help that is always in the way.
[/question]
