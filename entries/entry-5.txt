CoTrace is my conceptual AI learning platform, built on generativist pedagogy and structured around three phases: Explore, Draft and Refine. My Practical Project 2 proposes an orientation programme for newly appointed CoTrace facilitators. This entry began before that proposal was finalised, so some early ideas below differ from what I submitted, and I have mentioned them here to show how my thinking changed. My learner analysis showed three gaps in facilitators' contexts, and I wanted to see how Universal Design for Learning (UDL) could close them.

## Why engagement matters most here
Of the three principles in the diagram, engagement stood out the most because, beyond technology, the main challenge facilitators potentially face is affective and tied to identity. These experienced educators are being asked to shift from “knowledge deliverer” to “cognitive orchestrator” (Vendrell and Johnston, 2026), giving up the expert role Biggs and Tang (2007) describe, which can threaten their pedagogical agency and leave them unsure of their role.

The engagement principle (Ralabate, 2011) responds with a climate of trust, where early errors and technical breakdowns count as learning opportunities (Biggs and Tang, 2007). This is built into the human feedback facilitators receive, which also models how to respond when their own students make mistakes. It also offers tasks tied to facilitators’ real jobs and motivations (Khalil and Elkhider, 2016), so the orientation matters to people who already see themselves as skilled. Both choices reduce the same thing: uncertainty about what their role is now.

## Action, expression and representation
Facilitators come from different professional backgrounds, with assessment habits built around judging finished work (Corbin et al., 2025), and one standard assessment could create barriers. UDL’s answer is to let them show competence in different ways (Ralabate, 2011). My early ideas included authoring a Socratic prompt exercise or recording a simulated micro-teaching session.

Representation addresses the digital literacy gap, where AI literacy is likely to be uneven (Ní Uanachain and Aouad, 2025). Dense text would overload less tech-savvy facilitators (Moreno and Mayer, 2007), and it was decided that the design would include short video walkthroughs, visual workflow diagrams, and an on-screen pedagogical agent to guide facilitators.

## What changed, and why
My early feedback idea was rich multimodal feedback on a micro-teaching session. However, in the submitted design, facilitators get an automated first pass and a human comment within 24 hours. The reasons for this were due to reviewer workload, the identity and nuance that facilitators need from a person, and the respect and safety of human feedback. The choice of format survived only in Unit 4, where facilitators can submit their commentary as written, audio, or video. That is less than I imagined, but I decided to keep the choice in one unit and make it work rather than try to spread it across all four. This could always be adjusted in future iterations based on facilitator interactions and feedback.

## Final thoughts
UDL gave me a way to close the gaps I identified, and building the design showed me which of those ideas could survive real constraints. A good design idea has to hold up against workload, time, and the needs of the people using it, and mine did that by being scaled back to what could realistically be delivered. Applying UDL also made me question what happens when its flexibility meets the limits CTML places on cognitive load. That is where my next entry begins.
