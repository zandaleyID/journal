Where cognitive load theory and Universal Design for Learning pull in different directions.

CTML and CATLM are mostly about managing cognitive load. UDL starts from a different question, which is who can get in and how (CAST, 2024). Putting the three side by side showed me that they do not always agree. Three tensions stood out: redundancy versus multiple representation, coherence versus engagement, and efficiency versus choice.

I built the page below as an interactive diagram, so you can see each tension shift rather than just read about it.

One possible bridge, from Fadel (2008), is to give learners control over how they access content: toggling captions, adjusting the presentation or opting into pre-training. That meets UDL’s access goal without bringing back unnecessary load for everyone. I think of the three theories this way: CTML is the lens for cognitive architecture, CATLM extends it to cognitive-affective interaction, and UDL is the broader, inclusive design framework. It draws on CATLM’s affective ideas without collapsing into them (Moreno and Mayer, 2007; Ralabate, 2011).

Meeting the tension in my own design

I ran into this tension in PP2. I had drafted five learning outcomes for the orientation module, and each one held real value. Looking at the whole layout, something felt off, and once I put myself in the facilitators’ position it was clear: it was too much.

These facilitators were already navigating a new platform and an identity shift from content deliverer to cognitive orchestrator (Vendrell and Johnston, 2026). A task-heavy orientation on top risked the cognitive overload CTML warns about and the disengagement CATLM predicts (Moreno and Mayer, 2007). The value of each activity risked going unrealised, the same wasted affordance I noted in my Coursera critique (Day and Lloyd, 2007). Merging two assessments and making the journal ungraded cut that extraneous load while keeping each outcome’s intent (Mayer, 2024).

Final thoughts

Putting CTML and UDL side by side showed me that “good design” depends on the question you start with. Each theory fixes a problem the other can create, so I stopped treating them as competing options. The more useful question became who gets to decide, the designer or the learner. Giving learners some control over captions, presentation and pre-training seemed to me a way to respect both, and my own overloaded draft showed me why that matters: good instructional design is not about how much valuable content you can include, but about how much a real learner, under real constraints, can actually carry. Even so, I am not sure how much choice is too much, and I would want to test it with real learners before relying on it.

A question for you

Have you ever been given so many options in an online course that it made learning harder? What would the right amount of choice have looked like?
