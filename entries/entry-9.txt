This entry looks at how I scaffolded feedback in my PP2 design, what I left out, and how the Unit 4 capstone guides the process while leaving the meaning open for facilitators.

## Who gives the feedback?
In PP2, the procedural task in Unit 1 gets immediate AI-generated feedback, which gives formative knowledge without overloading human reviewers. Units 2 to 4 need analysis and authentic evaluation, so a human reviewer gives qualitative feedback.

[table]
caption: Table 1. Who gives feedback in each unit of my PP2 design (Bothma, 2026). Table generated by Claude (Anthropic, 2026). 
| Unit | Task | AI feedback | Human feedback |
| Unit 1 | Platform Navigation Task | Automated feedback on the platform's own summary screen | No separate review |
| Unit 2 | Mock Student Response Task | First-pass check against the procedural rubric | A fuller comment on the quality of guiding and questioning |
| Unit 3 | Holistic Portfolio Evaluation Task | Confirms completion only | Feedback on the depth of process |
| Unit 4 | Task Design Worksheet and reflective commentary | Confirms completion | Feedback on a personal, identity-related task |
[/table]

One reason for this feedback structure was learner perception. Wang et al. (2013) found that students adapt their learning approaches in response to the teaching and learning environment. I inferred that automated feedback on a high-stakes task might make the assessment feel less significant. I also considered the personal insight and nuance facilitators need from human feedback, particularly when respect and psychological safety are important. Additionally, through the Community of Inquiry framework, deciding who provides feedback and when is also part of teaching presence, which includes the design, facilitation, and direction of the course (Anderson et al., 2001, cited in Bosch, Laubscher and Olivier, 2020, p. 56).

## What I left out
What I never specified was how facilitators would use the human feedback they received. I assumed it would inform their work in the next unit or help correct misunderstandings, but feedback needs to lead to some form of action to become an active part of learning (Biggs and Tang, 2007). A simple improvement would be to add a low-stakes feedback response to the ungraded reflective journal. This would give facilitators an opportunity to engage with the feedback without adding another graded task or increasing their workload, because it belongs to their own exploration of the identity shift.

I also see now that the agent works as a collaborator, since facilitators can use it to discuss theories, test assumptions, and brainstorm. I intended that, but I didn't make it explicit in the wireframe.

## Guiding the process, leaving the meaning open
Thinking about what facilitators do with feedback, and how the agent supports them, led me to the Unit 4 capstone, which is where I most deliberately balance structure with freedom. Facilitators use a worksheet to design an open-ended learning task of their own, and then record a short reflective commentary on their design choices. The screen below is annotated to show which parts are fixed and which are left open.

## Final thoughts
Looking at the feedback structure and the Unit 4 worksheet together, I see the same decision. In both, I guide the process by choosing who gives feedback and when and by building in a moment where direct answers are held back, and I leave the meaning open to the facilitators, including what the shift from content deliverer to cognitive orchestrator means for them (Vendrell and Johnston, 2026). Guiding the process protects the pedagogy and keeps the load manageable, while leaving the meaning open respects facilitators as professionals. What I left out, a place for facilitators to use their feedback, showed me that structure only supports learning when it leads somewhere. I want to carry that into my future designs.
